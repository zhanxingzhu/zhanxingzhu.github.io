One update or one per step: how RL and SFT teach a transformer a chain of thought

Zhanxing Zhu · Joint work with Bochen Lyu, Yiyang Jia and Xiaohao Cai

ICML paperPDFarXivOpenReview

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) both teach language models to write out intermediate steps, a chain of thought, before they answer[1]. Do they teach it in the same way? In our ICML 2026 paper we answer this for a one-layer transformer that learns sparse Boolean functions such as parity. Both methods provably learn the task, but in a different order: RL with a reward for every step learns the whole chain in one update, while SFT on the model’s own steps learns it one step per update. What sets the order is not RL or SFT as such, but whether each step gets feedback that fits the state the model is trained on.

1. A task that needs intermediate steps

Why does parity need a chain of thought?

We study functions of many bits that depend on only a few of them. Parity is the hardest of these to learn in one go, but it splits into a tree of two-bit steps, as in earlier work on sub-task decomposition[3][4] (Fig. 1).

2. What the transformer has to learn

What must the model get right at each step?

The model is a single attention layer that writes the chain token by token and feeds each token back in. For every token, learning comes down to finding the two positions it should read (Fig. 2).

3. Four ways to give feedback

What feedback does each step of the chain receive?

RL scores the model’s own sampled chains with rewards, and SFT compares them with labels. Within each, one choice decides what a step learns from: rewards for every step or for the answer only, and correct or self-generated previous steps (Fig. 3).

4. One signed update moves attention onto the children

Why can a single update be enough?

Our analysis reduces learnability to one quantity, the critical gradient component. If, in expectation, it is larger at a token’s two children than at any other position, a signed gradient step moves that token’s attention straight towards the children (Fig. 4).

5. All at once, or one step at a time

In which order does each kind of feedback teach the steps?

Given feedback on every step, RL and SFT both meet the separation condition, for parity, AND and OR alike. What differs is how many steps meet it at each update (Fig. 5).

6. RL’s first gradient already points at every child

Does a trained transformer follow the theory under RL?

We trained the transformer on parity of 16 bits out of 20, a chain of four steps, with 50,000 training samples. At initialisation, the sign of the RL policy gradient already matches the target attention in steps 1, 2 and 3, and step 4 has nothing to learn (Fig. 6).

7. SFT’s gradient moves on one step per update

And under SFT on the model’s own steps?

On the same task, the SFT gradient is non-zero for one step at a time, in order: step 1 at the first update, step 2 at the second, step 3 at the third (Fig. 7).

The order does not come from RL or SFT as such. Give SFT correct prefixes and it learns every step at once, like RL with process rewards; give RL rewards whose labels come from the input rather than from its own previous step, and it turns step-wise like SFT (App. H). What matters is whether each step’s feedback is informative for the state the model is trained on, so comparisons such as “SFT memorizes, RL generalizes”[7] should state how supervision and trajectories are coupled. Our analysis is for a one-layer transformer with a structured mask; multi-layer models, realistic reasoning tasks and finite samples come next.

8. The setting, formally

Which functions, which model, and which target?

The functions. Inputs x are uniform on {−1, +1}d. A k-sparse Boolean function Φk depends only on the bits in a hidden set B ⊆ [d] of size k, with 2 ≤ k ≤ d (Def. 2.1). The paper takes k = 2T and functions that a complete binary tree of one fixed two-bit function ϕ2 computes level by level (Eq. 1):

y(t)j = ϕ2(y(t−1)c1(j), y(t−1)c2(j)),  t = 1, …, T,  j = 1, …, dt = k/2t,  y(0) = x(1)

Step t of the chain has dt tokens, the last token y(T)1 is Φk(x), and c1(j), c2(j), the children of token j, are its relevant positions. Table 1 of the paper pairs each function with an activation ψ:

functionϕ2(z1, z2)ψ(z)
k-PARITYz1z2z2
k-AND(z1z2 + z1 + z2 − 1)/2max(z, 0)
k-OR(−z1z2 + z1 + z2 + 1)/2min(z, 0) + 1

The model. One attention layer reads the sequence z = (x, y), whose k − 1 chain tokens start at 0, with one-hot positional encodings (Eq. 2). Keys and queries are merged into one matrix W that acts on positions only, and the value reads the token itself, so attention chooses positions and the layer returns a weighted average of token values; a feed-forward layer reduced to the fixed activation ψ turns that average into the output. For a token of step t with children c1 and c2, write Wp and σp for its score and its attention on the p-th token of step t − 1 (Eq. 3):

ξ = dt−1∑p=1 σp y(t−1)p,  σp = eWp ∑q eWq,  output ψ(ξ)(3)

Under RL, ψ(ξ) is the probability of writing +1; under SFT, q̂ = 2ψ(ξ) − 1 is a score and the token written is its sign (Eq. 10). Besides the causal mask, a fixed “pretrained” mask lets step t read only step t − 1, and step 1 only x: a structural prior standing in for pretraining. Every token has its own column of scores, initialised at W(0) = 1, so every token starts with attention 1/dt−1 on each position it can see. The best reward and the lowest loss are reached exactly when every token puts ½ on each child and 0 elsewhere; this attention, softmax(W⋆), is the target.

9. Three objectives, one kind of update

What does each method optimise, and how is it updated?

RL with process rewards. The model samples a whole chain y ∼ pW(·|x), and each step is scored against what is correct given the model’s own previous step (Eq. 5):

R(W) = 𝔼x 𝔼y∼pW [ T∑t=1 rt ],  rt = 1k − 1 dt∑j=1 y(t)j ȳ(t)j(5)

Here ȳ(t)j is ϕ2 of the model’s own tokens at the two children of token j. The exact policy gradient credits step t with all rewards from t onwards (Lemma E.1); the analysis uses the per-step form, which credits each step with its own reward, the one that judges it (Eq. 6). Updates keep only the sign of the gradient:

∇WR = 𝔼 [ T∑t=1 ∇W ln pW(y(t) | y(t−1)) rt ],  W(s + 1) = W(s) + η sign(∇WR)(6)

RL with a final reward. Only the answer is scored: rF = y(T) Φk(x), and the objective is RF(W) = 𝔼[rF].

SFT without teacher forcing. The model writes the whole chain itself, ŷ(t)j = sign(q̂(t)j), each token conditioned on its own earlier tokens, and every step is compared with the label ỹ(t) computed from x by the hinge loss ℓ(q̂, a) = max(0, 1 − q̂a) (Eqs. 10 and 11):

L(W) = 𝔼x T∑t=1 Lt,  Lt = 1k − 1 dt∑j=1 ℓ(q̂(t)j, ỹ(t)j),  W(s + 1) = W(s) − η sign(∇WL)(11)

Both methods take signed steps, a special case of Adam[8], from the same start, which makes them directly comparable. With teacher forcing, SFT would instead generate every step from the true previous step ỹ(t−1) (App. B, Q4).

10. The critical gradient component

Which part of the gradient decides what each token learns?

For a token of step t with children c1 and c2, the critical gradient component at position p is (Eq. 7)

γp(y(t−1)) = 2k − 1 ψ′(ξ) ϕ2(y(t−1)c1, y(t−1)c2) y(t−1)p,  p = 1, …, dt−1(7)

It correlates the value at position p with the correct output, weighted by the slope of ψ. The gradient on the token’s scores is a centred, attention-weighted version of it (Lemma E.2):

∂R∂Wp = 𝔼 [ ( γp −  ∑q σq γq ) σp ](19)

Two facts follow. The gradient sums to zero over p, because adding a constant to all scores leaves the softmax unchanged. And while the scores are still equal, as at W(0), every σp is 1/dt−1, so the gradient is larger at p than at p′ exactly when 𝔼[γp − γp′] > 0 (Eqs. 20 and 21). The separation condition asks this for every child p and every other position p′:

RL:  𝔼x, y(:t−1)∼pW [ γp(y(t−1)) − γp′(y(t−1)) ] > 0(8)
SFT:  𝔼x [ γp(ỹ(t−1)) − γp′(ỹ(t−1)) ] > 0(12)

The two conditions differ only in the previous step they are evaluated on: the model’s own samples under RL, the ground truth under SFT. Under separation, symmetry gives the gradient one value at both children and one at all other positions, and the zero sum makes the first positive and the second negative (Fig. 8).

A signed step discards the magnitudes. From W(0) = 1 it moves both children to 1 + η and every other position to 1 − η, which gives (Eqs. 64 and 66)

σc = 12 + (dt−1 − 2) e−2η,  σp′ = 1dt−1 − 2 + 2e2η,  ‖σ − σ⋆‖1 = 1½ + e2η/(dt−1 − 2) ≤ (dt−1 − 2) e−2η

One update with η ≥ ½ ln(d/ε) therefore brings every token whose gradient has this sign pattern within ε of its target. This is where the η = Ω(ln(d/ε)) of the theorems comes from.

11. RL: the whole chain in one update

What do the RL results guarantee, and where do they stop?

Theorem 3.1 (RL with process rewards)Let d ≥ k ≥ 2, W(0) = 1 and η = Ω(ln(d/ε)) for ε > 0. If the separation condition (8) holds for every step t, every token of it, every child p and every other position p′, then one signed policy-gradient update W(1) = W(0) + η sign(∇WR) gives ‖softmax(W(1)) − softmax(W⋆)‖1 ≤ ε.

Proof idea. Each token has its own column of scores, and the process reward for step t depends only on step t and the model’s own step t − 1. Condition (8) is thus a statement about W(0) for every step at once, however wrong the earlier steps still are. Where it holds, every token’s gradient has the sign pattern of Fig. 8, and the signed step of Section 10 moves all columns in the same update (App. E.3).

For parity the condition can be checked in closed form. At W(0), the gradient gap between a child and any other position is proportional to (1 − c2)((dt−1 − 3)c2 + 1)/d2t−1, which is positive because the tokens of step t − 1 share a mean c with |c| < 1 (Eq. 99, Lemma G.1). The separation persists at every iteration, so after s signed updates (Theorem 4.1)

σc(s) = 12 + (dt−1 − 2) e−2ηs,  σp′(s) = 1dt−1 − 2 + 2e2ηsThm. 4.1

A large step or many small ones reach the target; Fig. 4 plots s = 1. AND and OR satisfy condition (8) as well, with their ψ from Table 1 (Claim 4.2).

Proposition 3.2 (a final reward is hard for parity)Let ℋ be a set of functions h: {−1, +1}d → {−1, +1} with 𝔼x[h(x)h′(x)] = 0 for any two distinct h, h′, and let the model satisfy 𝔼x‖∇W 𝔼y∼pW[y(T)]‖2 ≤ M. With the final reward rFh = y(T)h(x), the policy gradient barely depends on which h is the target:
𝔼h∈ℋ ‖∇WRFh − 𝔼h′∈ℋ ∇WRFh′‖2 ≤ M|ℋ|(9)

All parities on d bits form such a set, with |ℋ| = 2d: for a target drawn from it, the part of the gradient that depends on the target is exponentially small in d, the classic argument against learning parity end to end[2]. This is a hardness result for this reward, not a learning schedule.

12. SFT: one step per update

Why can SFT not learn step t before step t − 1?

Theorem 3.3 (SFT without teacher forcing)Let d ≥ k ≥ 2, W(0) = 1 and η = Ω(ln(d/ε)). If the separation condition (12) holds for every step t, every token of it, every child p and every other position p′, then T signed gradient steps on the loss (11) give ‖softmax(W(T)) − softmax(W⋆)‖1 ≤ ε.

The SFT gradient for step t couples a token’s fixed label with what the model itself wrote at step t − 1. While the token’s scores are equal, the descent direction at p exceeds that at p′ by (1/dt−1)[γp(ŷ(t−1)) − γp′(ŷ(t−1))], where γ is built from the written tokens ŷ(t−1) and the label ỹ(t) (Lemma F.1, Eq. 54). The gap has the sign pattern of Fig. 8 when (i) the token’s scores are still equal, (ii) step t − 1 is written correctly, ŷ(t−1) = ỹ(t−1), and (iii) condition (12) holds (Lemma F.2).

Condition (ii) is the one that fails at first (Fig. 9). At W(0) every token of step 1 averages the same inputs with the same weights, so all of them write the same value (Eq. 75). A token of step 2 then sees one value at all its positions, γp does not depend on p, and its gradient is exactly zero for every input; the same holds for every later step. The sign of zero is zero, so these scores stay equal and condition (i) still holds when their turn comes. Step 1 reads x, which is always correct, so update 1 teaches step 1. From then on step 1 writes ỹ(1), condition (ii) holds for step 2 and update 2 teaches it, and so on up to step T (App. F.1). A step that has been learned keeps only a gradient of order δ = ½ − σc, which the analysis neglects (Eq. 74).

Three variants locate the cause. With teacher forcing, every step reads the true previous step from the start, so condition (ii) holds for all steps at the first update and SFT learns the whole chain at once (App. B, Q4). RL whose step rewards use labels computed from x, rather than from the model’s own previous step, learns step by step; App. H solves these dynamics exactly for chains of one and two steps, which rules out sampled versus deterministic generation as the cause. And a logistic or exponential loss in place of the hinge loss keeps the step-wise schedule (App. F.2). Condition (12) holds for parity (Claim 4.1) and for AND and OR (Claim 4.2).

13. What the assumptions buy

Which assumptions carry the results, and how far do they reach?

The model is deliberately minimal (App. B, Q7): one layer and one head; keys and queries merged into a matrix that acts on positions only; a value that reads the token; a feed-forward layer reduced to an activation matched to the target (Table 1); and the “pretrained” mask, under which each step reads only the previous one. The gradients are population gradients, the updates are signed and start from equal scores, and the conditions are sufficient rather than necessary. Learning should fail where separation fails, as it does with a final reward for parity.

The tree is not essential (App. I). For a serial chain y1 = ϕ2(xi1, xi2), yj = ϕ2(yj−1, xij+1), with a mask that lets token j read all of x and the previous token, the same separation argument holds: RL with process rewards and SFT with teacher forcing learn the whole chain in one update. SFT without teacher forcing then needs a filter that holds back the loss of later tokens until the earlier ones are learned; without it, later tokens get noisy gradients and learning is hard (App. I.2).

Multi-layer and multi-head models, realistic reasoning tasks, finite samples, noisy optimisation and imperfect rewards are left open (App. A).
