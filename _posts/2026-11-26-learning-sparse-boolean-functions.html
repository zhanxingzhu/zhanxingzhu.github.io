RL and SFT both teach a transformer to reason step by step, but not in the same order

Zhanxing Zhu · Joint work with Bochen Lyu, Yiyang Jia and Xiaohao Cai

Read the ICML 2026 paper

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) can both teach a model to write out intermediate steps, or a chain of thought[1]. In our ICML 2026 paper, we ask which steps the model learns first. For a one-layer transformer on a family of sparse Boolean problems, we prove that RL with a reward for every step learns the whole chain in one update. When we train the same model with SFT on its own outputs, it learns one step per update, starting with the first.

1. A task that needs intermediate steps

Why does learning parity need a chain of thought?

We study functions of a long bit string that depend on only a few hidden bits. Parity is the hardest of them to learn directly (Fig. 1).

2. What the transformer has to learn

What exactly changes in the model when it learns the task?

The model is a single attention layer that writes the steps one token at a time, reading what is already there. Learning comes down to where each token looks (Fig. 2).

3. Four ways to supervise the chain

Where does each training method get its feedback?

RL and SFT are not two set-ups but four, depending on what is rewarded and on whose earlier steps the model reads during training (Fig. 3).

4. Same chain, different order of learning

Why does RL learn every step at once and SFT one at a time?

Early in training the model’s own steps are wrong. What matters is whether the feedback for a step still makes sense when the step before it is wrong (Fig. 4).

5. The gradient, update by update

What does the training signal look like in each case?

The proofs rest on one quantity: whether the gradient pushes attention up at a token’s two children and down everywhere else. Laid out as a grid, the difference between the two methods can be seen directly (Fig. 5).

6. The experiment shows the same pattern

Does a trained model actually behave this way?

We tested this prediction on parity with 20 input bits, 16 of them relevant, so the chain has four steps (Fig. 6).

7. It is the feedback, not the method

So is the difference really RL versus SFT?

Putting the four settings side by side gives the paper’s main message (Fig. 7).

Our results suggest two questions to ask when comparing RL with SFT[6]. Which steps receive feedback? During training, does the model read its own earlier steps or the correct ones? Both choices affect how the model learns the chain.
