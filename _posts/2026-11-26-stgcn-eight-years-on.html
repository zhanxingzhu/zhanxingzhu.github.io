Learning dynamics on a graph: how STGCN forecasts many coupled time series at once

Zhanxing Zhu · Joint work with Bing Yu and Haoteng Yin (IJCAI 2018)

arXivPDFIJCAI proceedingsCodePyTorch port

Many real signals are measured at points that are linked to each other: sensors on roads, stations in a city, electrodes on a scalp. The right object is then a graph whose every node carries a time series. In our IJCAI 2018 paper, we introduced the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN) for forecasting these coupled time series. To our knowledge, it was the first model for this problem built entirely from convolutions over the graph and over time. We tested it on road sensors. Its sandwich block became a standard building block of graph neural networks for time series[7], and the paper is among the most cited in that field.

1. A network of time series

What is forecast, and why draw it as a graph?

Each sensor reports a speed every five minutes, and nearby sensors move together. We treat the readings at one moment as a signal on a graph. Stacking these signals in time lets us forecast the next few frames (Fig. 1).

2. Where earlier models fall short

What do per-series models, grid CNNs and RNNs miss?

Before this paper, forecasters either ignored the network, forced it onto a grid, or read time with recurrent chains. STGCN keeps the graph as it is and reads time with convolutions instead, building on spectral graph convolution[1][2] and convolutional sequence models[3] (Fig. 2).

3. Convolution over the graph

How does a sensor read its neighbours?

A graph has no left and right, so we define our filter through the graph Laplacian and approximate it with polynomials, after Defferrard et al.[1] and Kipf and Welling[2]. In practice a node mixes its own value with those of nodes a few edges away (Fig. 3).

4. Convolution over time, with a gate

How is the time axis read without recurrence?

Along the time axis, we use a plain one-dimensional convolution with a gate, the gated linear unit of Gehring et al.[3]. Every output depends only on a short window of the past, so all positions are computed at once (Fig. 4).

5. The sandwich block and the full network

How do the two convolutions fit together?

The ST-Conv block puts one graph convolution between two temporal ones, and the model stacks two such blocks before an output layer. Each temporal layer shortens the stack of frames by two, so twelve frames come out as one forecast (Fig. 5).

6. Accuracy on 228 and 1,026 sensors

Does it beat the methods it replaces, at every horizon?

On the California highway data (PeMSD7), both STGCN variants have the lowest error at all three horizons, and the gap grows with the horizon (Fig. 6).

7. Training cost

What does the convolutional design buy in time and size?

Because no step waits for the one before, training runs in parallel. We measured the full training time against GCGRU on both PeMSD7 graphs (Fig. 7).

8. Eight years of citations

How widely has the idea been taken up?

Citations of the paper have grown every year since it appeared, and the official code is still maintained. The counts differ between databases, so the figure shows them side by side (Fig. 8).

9. The family it started

What grew from the sandwich block, and where did it travel?

Later models kept the pairing of graph convolution and temporal convolution and changed one part at a time: a learned graph[5], attention, continuous time. The decisive step for the general case was MTGNN[6], which learns the graph from the series themselves, so no map is needed (Fig. 9).

In the paper, we reported that STGCN outperformed the state of the art on two real-world data sets while achieving “faster training, easier convergences, and fewer parameters”, and that the framework “can be applied into more general spatio-temporal structured sequence forecasting scenarios, such as evolving of social networks, and preference prediction in recommendation systems”. The field did exactly that, and the block now reads air-quality stations, EEG electrodes and dengue counts that never stood beside a road[9].
